# Physics paper

**Purpose.** Convince physicists that a result about nature, or about a model, is true and matters. The reader asks, in order: what did you find? Why should I care? Why should I believe it? What can I do with it?

## Structure

1. **Title.** States the result or the question, not the topic. "Pairing gaps close linearly at the topological transition" beats "On topological superconductivity".
2. **Abstract (100-200 words).** Problem in one sentence, what was done in one, the main result with its number or scaling, why it matters. No symbols unless unavoidable, no citations.
3. **Introduction.** A narrowing funnel, about one page:
   - the physical question and why it is open, made specific within two paragraphs;
   - what is known and exactly where it stops, citing the work that defines the gap;
   - what we do and what we find, with the key number or scaling;
   - optionally, one or two sentences on the organisation, only if the paper is long.
   Many readers stop after the introduction, so it must contain the result.
4. **Setup or model.** Only what the results need. Motivate each ingredient physically ("we include X because without it Y"). State approximations and their small parameters here.
5. **Results.** Ordered by importance or by logic, not by the order in which they were obtained. Each subsection opens with its result. Figures carry the evidence; the text says what each figure shows and what it means.
6. **Discussion.** Physical interpretation, the regime of validity, comparison with experiment and prior work, the most important limitation, and what follows (a prediction, a test, an open question). Not a summary of the results section.
7. **Appendices.** Long derivations, numerical details and convergence checks. The main text says what each appendix establishes.

## Tone and level of detail

- Physics-level rigor (the `rigor` skill, level P). Standard formal steps are fine; correctness, limits, regimes and orders of magnitude are not optional.
- Physical intuition first, then the formula. Give scaling arguments and estimates before exact results.
- Mathematical detail in the main text only where it carries the argument. A reader should be able to follow the logic while skipping every derivation.
- State uncertainty in physical terms: error bars, the size of the next order, the regime. Not modal verbs.
- Compare with experiment or established results wherever possible; it is the strongest evidence a physics reader accepts.
- Name the approximation that limits the result, once, in the place where it matters.

## Common failures

- An introduction that surveys the field for a page before stating the question.
- Results presented in the order they were computed.
- Equations without a sentence saying what they mean physically.
- A discussion that repeats the results.
- Claims of generality that the model does not support.
